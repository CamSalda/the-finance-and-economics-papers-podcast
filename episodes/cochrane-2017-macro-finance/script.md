Then he tells you which one he likes, and why he thinks the others are worse.

## Two facts and one question

MEI: Macro-finance, in his definition, studies the link between asset prices and economic fluctuations. And it rests on a few facts. Fact one: stocks have a much higher average return than bonds. The equity premium, the gap between the two, is somewhere between four and eight percent a year.

MIGUEL: And the standard answer is that stocks are risky.

MEI: Which Cochrane says is not an answer. People buy lottery tickets and go to casinos. So stocks must have a special kind of risk: they fall at particularly inconvenient times.

MIGUEL: Like when you lose your job.

MEI: Exactly. The canonical model says the expected excess return of an asset is minus its covariance with the stochastic discount factor, the growth of marginal utility. In words: assets that lose money when investors are already hungry must pay a premium. The simplest version makes marginal utility a function of consumption. Stocks fall when consumption falls, so they are feared.

MIGUEL: Sounds reasonable. Where does it break?

MEI: In the numbers. The Sharpe ratio of the stock market, average excess return over volatility, is about 0.25 to 0.5. In the model, that ratio is at most risk aversion times the volatility of consumption growth. And consumption growth has a standard deviation of only one or two percent a year.

MIGUEL: So you need risk aversion of 25 or more to make the numbers match.

MEI: And then the risk-free rate blows up. With risk aversion of 25 and consumption growing at one or two percent, the model wants real interest rates of 25 to 50 percent. That's the equity premium puzzle and the risk-free rate puzzle, together.

MIGUEL: Okay, that's fact one. Fact two?

MEI: Risk premiums move over time, and they move with the business cycle. You can forecast stock returns with the price-dividend ratio. Bond returns with yield spreads. Currency returns with interest rate differences.

MIGUEL: I've heard those regressions have tiny R-squareds.

MEI: They do, at one-month or one-year horizons. But Cochrane says look at the economic size, not the t-statistic. If the equity premium is six percent on average, at any given moment it is just as likely to be one percent or eleven percent. Expected returns vary over time as much as their level.

MIGUEL: Eleven percent in a recession and one percent at the top of a boom.

MEI: Right. Expected returns are high and prices are low at the bottom of recessions, across many asset classes at once. And here is a point he loves to make. Shiller famously showed that stock prices are too volatile relative to dividends. Cochrane says that is the same fact as predictability. High prices must mean either higher future dividends or lower future returns. If they don't predict dividends, they must predict returns.

MIGUEL: So "excess volatility" and "return predictability" are two names for one thing.

MEI: Two names for one thing. So the field faces two questions. One: what is it about recessions that makes people so afraid of stocks falling then? That's the equity premium question. Two: what is it about recessions that makes people even more afraid going forward, so the premium rises? That's the predictability question.

MIGUEL: Are those not the same question?

MEI: He insists they are separate. You could hate recessions but not become more risk averse during them. Power utility is like that: losses hurt, but they don't change your appetite for the next bet. Recessions seem to combine both, pain now and more fear about the future.

## One shape for every model

MIGUEL: Okay. So what did the profession come up with?

MEI: He lists ten approaches, with a flagship paper for each. Habits. Recursive utility. Long-run risks. Idiosyncratic risk. Heterogeneous preferences. Rare disasters. Utility that is not separable across goods. Leverage and institutional finance. Ambiguity aversion. And behavioral finance.

MIGUEL: Ten theories. And he says they are all the same?

MEI: Here's the claim. Every one of them boils down to the same discount factor: the usual power-utility term, consumption growth raised to minus risk aversion, multiplied by one extra variable. He calls it Y. And, quote, "The new variable Y does most of the work."

MIGUEL: So each theory is just a different story about what Y is.

MEI: That's the whole paper in one sentence. In the habit model, Y is how far consumption is above a habitual level. In long-run risk, Y is news about consumption far in the future. In idiosyncratic risk models, Y is how unequal consumption growth is across people. In rare disaster models, Y is the probability of a catastrophe. In institutional models, Y is the health of the banks' balance sheets.

MIGUEL: Even the behavioral models? Those are about people being wrong, not about marginal utility.

MEI: Even those. In the pricing equation, the probability of each state and the marginal utility in that state are always multiplied together. You can never tell them apart from prices alone. So a distorted probability acts exactly like a distorted marginal utility. A sentiment variable is just another Y.

MIGUEL: That's a little deflating. Ten schools of thought, one equation.

MEI: And then he adds what he thinks matters for which models win. Not test statistics. Tractability, elegance, and the number of extra assumptions you need. He borrows a phrase from Chen, Dou and Kogan: the extra assumptions are "dark matter." Models that need an extra assumption for every fact are less convincing than models that tie several facts together with few assumptions.

MIGUEL: Dark matter. Stuff you can't see but need to make the universe work.

MEI: And there's a line I have to read you. He warns that finance is "always in danger of being simply an interpretive or poetic discipline." Quote: "Markets went down, sentiment must have fallen. Markets went down, risk aversion must have risen. Markets went down, there must have been selling pressure. Markets went down, the Gods must be displeased."

MIGUEL: The Gods must be displeased. I'm putting that in my next referee report.

## Habits

MEI: Now his favorite, the habit model, from his 1999 paper with John Campbell in the Journal of Political Economy. The idea is simple. Utility depends not on consumption, but on consumption minus a habit level, which he also calls a subsistence point.

MIGUEL: So I don't care how much I eat. I care how much I eat compared to what I've gotten used to.

MEI: Exactly. And here's the trick. With that utility, risk aversion is no longer a constant. It equals the curvature parameter divided by the surplus consumption ratio, which is how far consumption sits above habit, as a fraction of consumption. When consumption falls toward habit, that ratio shrinks, and risk aversion rises.

MIGUEL: So the same risk feels scarier when I'm close to the edge.

MEI: And the habit moves slowly. As consumption rises, people slowly get used to the higher level. Then when consumption falls relative to that level, it hurts more than the same consumption did on the way up. He says he once overheard a hedge fund manager's spouse say at a cocktail party, quote, "I'd sooner die than fly commercial again."

MIGUEL: That is the most finance-conference sentence I've ever heard.

MEI: Now follow the chain. In a recession, consumption falls toward habit. Risk aversion rises. Expected returns rise. Higher expected returns mean lower prices relative to dividends. So a low price-dividend ratio forecasts high returns for a long time. And expected dividends are constant in the model, so every bit of movement in the price-dividend ratio comes from risk premiums.

MIGUEL: So it produces the excess volatility and the predictability at the same time.

MEI: And he gives you the human version. At the top of a boom, the investor knows expected returns are low. But times are good, they can afford some risk, and what else are they going to do with the money? They "reach for yield." At the bottom, after a crash, they know it's a buying opportunity. But, quote, "I might lose my job. If things get any worse, I could lose the house too. There is a minimum standard of living I just can't put at risk."

MIGUEL: That is the most honest description of 2009 I've heard.

MEI: And then a confession. Quote: "This habit model is proudly reverse-engineered." They started from the intuition and designed the preferences to deliver it. He adds a note to PhD students: "All good economic models are reverse-engineered! If you pour plausible sounding ingredients in the pot and stir, you will never get anywhere."

MIGUEL: Good to know. I've been stirring for two years.

MEI: Now the hard part, the risk-free rate. Habit models usually wreck it. When consumption is near habit, people want to borrow against a brighter future, which pushes interest rates up and down violently. Campbell and Cochrane engineered the habit so that another force cancels it: precautionary saving. In bad times people also want to save against a risky future. The two desires offset. In his words, in bad times "people want to borrow more against a better future, but they want to save more against a risky future, and in the end they do neither."

MIGUEL: How big is that precautionary term?

MEI: Huge. With a curvature of two and a surplus ratio of five percent, effective risk aversion is forty. He computes that precautionary saving then subtracts 48 percentage points from the risk-free rate. It's the thing that keeps rates low and stable.

MIGUEL: Forty-eight points of offset to get a two percent rate. That's a knife balanced on a knife.

MEI: He'd say it's engineering. So what does the model deliver? The equity premium, the Sharpe ratio, low consumption volatility, a low stable risk-free rate, predictability, excess volatility, and volatility that rises after a price fall. What it does not deliver is low risk aversion. The curvature is two, but effective risk aversion is enormous. So he says, bluntly, the habit model does not solve the equity premium puzzle as properly stated. And then: "So far no model has achieved a full solution of the equity premium puzzle as stated."

MIGUEL: Including his own. I respect that.

MIGUEL: And does it fit the data?

MEI: His Figure 3 plots the stock market's price-dividend ratio against consumption minus a moving average of past consumption, from 1950 to 2015. The two lines track each other, and he points out the correlation is stronger after 1999. The 2008 plunge, the 2010 recovery, even the slowdown of 2013 and 2014, all mirror consumption. His sentence: "The brickbats thrown at modern efficient-market finance for being unable to accommodate the financial crisis are simply false."

MIGUEL: Brickbats. He's enjoying himself.

## Long-run risk, and why he doesn't buy it

MIGUEL: Okay, the main rival. Long-run risk. I hear about it in every seminar.

MEI: It's the most popular alternative, built on recursive utility from Epstein and Zin, and developed by Bansal and Yaron. Recursive utility separates two things that power utility ties together: risk aversion, and the willingness to shift consumption over time. Then you substitute in a consumption process with a small, very persistent component in its growth rate.

MIGUEL: So a small change in consumption today means a big change in consumption far in the future.

MEI: And that long-run news is the Y. People fear stocks because stocks fall when there is bad news about consumption growth decades ahead. Not because the economy is in a recession today.

MIGUEL: And Cochrane's problem with it?

MEI: Several. The first is the data. The model needs a lot of news about long-run growth, and a lot of time variation in the uncertainty about it. But nondurable and services consumption, in his words, "looks awfully close to a random walk." If each day your forecast of consumption in 2030 is the same, the model collapses back to power utility.

MIGUEL: Can't you just say the standard errors are big and you can't rule it out?

MEI: You can. But he says that demotes the central ingredient from a robust fact to an assumption that is hard to falsify, which "clearly weakens the whole business."

MIGUEL: Fair.

MEI: The second problem is the story. He tells students to write the 900-word op-ed version of their paper. If you can't explain the idea to a lay audience, maybe it isn't such a good idea. And then he writes the op-ed for long-run risk. Why was fall 2008 so painful? Not because people lost jobs and houses. Those facts, per se, were irrelevant. It was because 2008 came with bad news about growth in the 2020s, which investors figured out before any professional forecaster did.

MIGUEL: That does sound strange when you say it out loud.

MEI: And why did university endowments sell in a panic in 2008, despite calling themselves long-run investors? Not because their cash flows were at risk. Because their uncertainty about conditions in 2028 had gone up. His verdict: "a difficult proposition to explain honestly to an MBA class on any day but the first of April."

MIGUEL: April Fools' Day. He's not subtle.

MEI: The deeper issue is the defining feature of recursive utility: a preference for resolving uncertainty early, even when you can do nothing with the information. He finds the lab evidence for it unpersuasive, because in daily life there is almost never news you can't act on. And he cites a calculation by Epstein, Farhi and Strzalecki: the consumer in the Bansal-Yaron economy would give up 20 to 30 percent of consumption just for the psychic pleasure of knowing the future in advance. "That seems like a lot."

MIGUEL: Twenty to thirty percent of my consumption to read tomorrow's newspaper today. I'd pay maybe two.

MEI: So he concludes the early-resolution feature "starts to look more like a bug than a feature." But, and this is important, he ends the section generously. Both models capture the same idea: an extra state variable tied to recessions. One uses time-varying risk aversion, the other time-varying risk. Neither is ahead on the number of facts explained. The habit model reverse-engineers preferences. Long-run risk reverse-engineers the consumption process. And, quote, "One observer's fragile assumption is another observer's well-identified parameter."

MIGUEL: That's the most diplomatic sentence in the paper.

MEI: He admits his preference for one over the other is "an esthetic judgment."

## The rest of the zoo

MIGUEL: Okay. We have eight more theories and limited time. Speed round?

MEI: Speed round. Idiosyncratic risk, Constantinides and Duffie, 1996. Y is the cross-sectional variance of consumption growth across people. When inequality in consumption growth spikes, marginal utility rises on average, because losses hurt more than gains help. He calls the paper "brilliant because it is so simple." But he says the empirical work has been "a bit disappointing": cross-sectional risk does rise in recessions, but not enough to generate the premiums we see, because people smooth consumption.

MIGUEL: Next.

MEI: Heterogeneous preferences, Garleanu and Panageas, 2015. The mirror image. Everyone's risks are perfectly insured, but people differ in risk aversion. The brave hold more stock, so when the market falls they lose more wealth, and the market as a whole tilts toward the timid. The representative investor becomes more risk averse after a crash even though no individual did. He calls it "a beautiful model."

MIGUEL: So the market gets scared because the fearless got poorer.

MEI: Nicely put. Next: debt, balance sheets and institutional finance, the models that boomed after 2008. Here the mechanism looks like habits with debt playing the role of the habit level. As income falls toward your debt, you take less risk. Banks "delever" and "repair balance sheets."

MIGUEL: That sounds very plausible. What's his objection?

MEI: Three. First, why would people get more risk averse near bankruptcy rather than less? Default is usually modeled as an option, and the standard worry is that firms near bankruptcy gamble too much. Second, net debt is zero. My debt is your asset. So these models need segmented markets, where only leveraged intermediaries trade. Third, and this is the big one, we are all marginal. Stocks and bonds sit in everyone's 401(k) through Vanguard and E-Trade. If banks dumped stocks at fire-sale prices, where were the endowments, the pension funds, the sovereign wealth funds? His answer: "they were selling in a panic like everyone else." That smells like rising risk aversion, not a segmented market.

MIGUEL: And the unconstrained rich?

MEI: Quote: "Bill Gates and the Harvard endowment have a lot more money than you and I do." The average person may be constrained, but the average dollar isn't. His verdict: institutional frictions are "surely important frosting on the macro-finance cake." Then: "But are they also the cake?"

MIGUEL: Frosting. Okay. Rare disasters?

MEI: Rietz in 1988, revived by Barro in 2006. People fear catastrophes that haven't shown up in the postwar sample, so marginal utility is more volatile in their heads than in our data. He finds the basic idea reasonable: Californians still buy earthquake insurance though the last big one was in 1906. But to get predictability you need time-varying disaster probabilities, which he calls not just dark matter but "dark energy." And if all price movements come from time-varying fears of once-a-century Armageddons, then nothing in finance is measurable. His line: "I prefer to go back and look for the car keys under the light."

MIGUEL: And the behavioral models?

MEI: This is a section worth reading in full. His point is that probability and marginal utility always enter the pricing equation together, so from prices alone you can't tell irrationality from risk aversion. That's Fama's joint hypothesis theorem from 1970. He's baffled that the profession kept fighting a battle that was proven undecidable fifty years ago. The way out is to tie sentiment to something measurable. He praises Barberis and co-authors' extrapolative model for doing exactly that.

MIGUEL: What about surveys where people expect crazy returns?

MEI: He has a lovely objection. Surveys ask what you "expect." But the price of an asset is the expected payoff under risk-neutral probabilities, which blend how likely an event is with how much it would hurt. He argues it's perfectly sensible for people to think in those terms. Avoid stubbing your toe, yes, but worry more about the truck, even though it's less likely.

MIGUEL: So when a survey respondent says they "expect" ten percent, they might be reporting a risk-neutral number.

MEI: And they'd have no idea what you meant if you asked. He adds that bees optimize beautifully when they search flowers for pollen, "yet they do not answer surveys coherently either."

## What to work on next

MIGUEL: He's torn everyone down. Does he build anything?

MEI: A research agenda, and some of it is self-criticism. The habit model has one shock and one state variable, so it predicts that everything moves in lockstep with the dividend yield. The data has at least two shocks, cash flows and discount rates, and many variables that forecast returns. His big open question: how many state variables for expected returns do we really need? Hundreds, or two or three?

MIGUEL: And the factor zoo?

MEI: He says macro-finance's job is to explain why the factor portfolios earn their premiums, since finance alone never can: the CAPM takes the equity premium as a free parameter. But he also warns theorists. Harvey, Liu and Zhu count 316 published factors. Many may be spurious. And he picks on one celebrated new fact, the term structure of equity risk premiums, where the key difference had t-statistics of 0.75 and 0.97. His advice: "Theorists, beware. Read empirical papers carefully before jumping to explain new facts."

MIGUEL: And formal testing of these models?

MEI: He thinks it is a dead end, and rightly dead. Every simplified model can be rejected at zero p-value, because a one-state-variable model predicts perfect correlations. The Fama-French three-factor model, the most useful model of 25 years, is "blown away" by the formal test. "If a glass is 95% full, that's an interesting fact even if you can prove it is not 100% full." So evaluating models is an art, and, quote, "Economics lives in the world of McCloskey, not Thomas Bayes or R. A. Fisher."

MIGUEL: Deirdre McCloskey, the rhetoric of economics. So persuasion, not p-values.

## Recessions are about fear

MEI: And the last section is the ambitious one. He says macro-finance has barely touched macroeconomics. Standard macro explains recessions with the interest rate: people consume less today because rates or patience changed. Macro-finance says the opposite. In 2008 people didn't become thrifty. They were, quote, "scared to death."

MIGUEL: So consumption fell because of risk, not because of the Fed funds rate.

MEI: Risk, risk aversion, precautionary saving. And it explains the puzzle Keynes started with: if people want to save more, why doesn't investment rise? His answer: because scared savers also shift from risky assets to safe ones. A flight to quality. So corporate borrowing costs rise while government rates fall, stock prices fall, and investment falls even though saving rises.

MIGUEL: So the recession is a risk premium, not an interest rate.

MEI: That's his sentence, almost word for word. And he notes that macro has adopted habits and recursive utility, but in small doses that kill the risk channel. "Macro has arrived at the banquet, but only sampled the appetizer."

MIGUEL: He really does like food metaphors.

MEI: Cake, frosting, banquet, appetizer, meat and vegetables. All in one paper.

## Wrap-up: the recap

MIGUEL: Okay, recap time. Give me the whole paper in one minute.

MEI: The question: what makes people so afraid of stocks falling in bad times, and why does that fear itself rise in recessions? Those are the equity premium question and the predictability question, and he insists they are distinct.

MIGUEL: The method?

MEI: A survey and an argument. He shows that ten different theories all reduce to the power-utility discount factor times one extra recession variable, Y. Then he compares them on three criteria: which facts they match, how measurable their Y is, and how much "dark matter" they need.

MIGUEL: Main takeaways?

MEI: One: excess volatility and return predictability are the same fact, and both say risk premiums vary with the business cycle. Two: no model, including his own habit model, has solved the equity premium puzzle as properly stated, with low risk aversion. Three: the candidates for Y are all highly correlated, so telling them apart from macro data is hard and maybe not that productive. Four: tie your extra variable to something measurable, or you are writing poetry about displeased gods.

MIGUEL: And the conclusion?

MEI: His favorite, habits, is in his words "at least not yet superseded," because its risk aversion is endogenous and measurable from consumption data. And the frontier is macroeconomics: recessions should be understood as episodes of high risk premiums and flight to quality, not of thrift and interest rates.

MIGUEL: My takeaway: ten theories, one equation, and the Gods are displeased. Also, write the 900-word version of your paper before the 40-page one.

MEI: And read the paper. It's the rare survey that is fun to read. Thanks for listening to The Finance and Economics Papers Podcast.

MIGUEL: See you next time.
